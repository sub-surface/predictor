It feels like roguelikes and roguelites got popular pretty much overnight. One minute, we had one every couple of years, then by the late 2010s, we were getting a new one every week. By design, this makes sense, as the repetitive nature of roguelikes naturally reduces development time and costs, but it is still quite astounding just how many there are.

Unfortunately, the amount of roguelikes often means less that there are Binding of Isaac or Balatro-level hits, and more a massive pile of roguelikes that just don’t catch on. Though while you could arguably say that about any genre, roguelikes are uniquely more fragile than most other genres. While you could play an RPG that missteps when it comes to its gunplay, or has a small string of quests that aren’t all that interesting, certain missteps in roguelikes can have a domino effect on everything else. Of course, there are smaller things that these games can be forgiven for, but generally, one screwup can spell disaster. That naturally begs the question: What does a roguelike need to get right to succeed?

While I’d never claim that my thoughts are the absolute (except that I am always correct and everyone who disagrees is wrong and dumb, of course), that’s the question I want to answer. To me, there are four core design pillars where if one gets messed up, it doesn’t matter if the other three pillars are great. It’s going to drag the game down, and it’s going to drag it down **hard.**

## Run Length

One of the greatest strengths behind roguelikes is their digestibility. RPGs, turn-based strategy games, soulslikes, and plenty of other genres require you to sit down for an hour at a time to properly enjoy them, if not longer. There’s no problem with this, but oftentimes you don’t have the energy or time to sit that long with a game. That’s where roguelikes can come in, often featuring much shorter runs where you can pick it up real quick, do a run or two, then put it back down and go about your day. It doesn’t feel like a huge time investment in the same way an online shooter like Marvel Rivals does (even if that game can be mentally taxing at times), so it’s much less daunting.

The best example I can think of immediately is Crypt of the NecroDancer. Thanks to its concept as a rhythm-based dungeon crawler, runs are often no more than 15 minutes. While I’ve done most of what I want to do with it, it’s still a game I want to pick up again once in a while just to do a quick run and jam to a killer soundtrack. Then I lose about three hours which goes against my point a touch, but it’s not the game’s fault that I have a problem on occasion. Regardless, it’s a bite-sized experience that can either serve as a quick break or fill an afternoon.

In contrast, we have a game like Everspace, which isn’t a bad roguelike on its own. A space combat flight sim, a niche genre that I have a soft spot for, is a unique idea for a roguelike and can make for a really fun game. The issue comes from how damn long runs can go if you get into the later stages. If you lose an hour into a run and are about three-quarters of the way to the finale, the idea of starting another run only to have to do that first hour again just doesn’t feel worth it. There’s a good reason I abandoned it after only a handful of runs. You can have a fun and unique gameplay loop, but if I’m going to have to take over an hour just to get back to the new stuff, I’m not going to bother.

## Run Difficulty

This may sound obvious given the nature of the genre, but if you’re going to make a roguelike, you need to make sure it’s difficult. You can make a wonderfully diverse roguelike with all these different great things, but if all of those are in service to runs that feel like you’re just breezing through, what’s the point? I couldn’t tell you the amount of roguelikes I’ve played for maybe five hours because I was just winning every run. Worse yet is when these roguelikes don’t understand the fine line between luck and skill, leaning one way or the other too heavily. If it’s all skill-based, everything else loses meaning in the face of just getting good. If it’s too luck-based, it’s going to feel less like you’re completing a run and more like you‘re pulling a slot machine in the desperate hope of getting three 7s.

If you’ll excuse me while I use one of my bingo free spaces, so to speak, Hades + Hades II understand this aspect perfectly. Certain Hammer abilities and god boons can make you an unstoppable monster as you tear through hordes of demons, but if you don’t have the game knowledge to dodge enemy attack patterns or are fighting a boss you don’t understand, you’re going to get your ass kicked. The right balance sees a roguelike let you get those coveted “god runs” through its passives, but never allows those to make the game a complete cakewalk. Only the combination of that and your own skill is going to see you through.

Contrasting that heavily is Dicey Dungeons, a game that I have an admittedly personal grudge with but I’m going to treat as kindly as possible. See, for the first few hours Dicey Dungeons is an excellent experience. The vibes are immaculate, the learning of all the different moves is great, and the premise is wholly unique. Play any longer though, and you’ll have hit the skill ceiling. What results is an array of runs that slowly become less a game of using the right moves, and more a game of waiting for the dice rolls to hit just right. Given there are nearly 30 hours of game time after that point, trying to get to the credits becomes a nightmare. I’m still not sure what led me to go through that, but I digress. The point is that if your game leans the scale one way too far, it’s going to result in an experience people don’t want to play. The fact I’m one of just 6.6% of players on Steam who completed Dicey Dungeons speaks volumes to that fact.

## Run Uniqueness

Roguelikes, by nature, are an incredibly repetitive genre. You’re doing the same thing over and over again, so by nature every roguelike has a certain shelf life. This means that if you want to keep players, you need to work hard to ensure that those runs feel unique and interesting. There needs to be variance in each core run to mask that repetition, or it’s going to become exhausting rather quickly. This could be done through new challenges in each run, variance in level design, ora bunch of unique characters.

A game that I find overcomes this issue well is Risk of Rain 2. Not only are the worlds you visit entirely randomized, but the wide array of characters all utilize certain passive items differently. While the Fungus is terrible on just about every other character, having them on the Engineer allows him to make his turrets create these huge healing AoEs. This not only keeps the Engi’s turrets alive, but helps allies too in the chaotic mess of battle since they’re bound to take massive amounts of damage. Given how detrimental a death is in RoR2, that can be a godsend. This goes for every character, making both discovering and getting the right passives can make some runs a slaughterfest in your favor.

By contrast, we have OTXO, an otherwise unique Hotline Miami clone turned roguelike. While this was super fun for its first few hours, the fact you always visit the same locations and use weapons that functionally don’t feel all that different can make it feel boring rather quickly. I can only take so much of the same thing before I’ve just had my fun and lose all motivation to go in for another run. Shame too, since OTXO’s soundtrack is otherwise fantastic (though ineffectively used, adding to that repetition).

## Interesting Passives

Last, but certainly not least (or least common, that’s for sure), are interesting passives. While this slightly plays into some of the other pillars, having passives that provide unique gameplay opportunities can elevate a roguelike and make each run not just feel unique, but play into buildcrafting perfectly. Think the difference between “10% increased damage” versus something like “dashes create damaging tornadoes along the path”. You want passives that actually change the experience, not simply tick up a stat sheet.

Image via the Balatro Steam page.

It’s finally time for my other bingo free space. Balatro understands the nature of Poker and creates passives that completely change the experience from run to run. And it covers each type of player perfectly. Some passives lean into flushes by either making them much easier or rewarding you for hitting them with increased mult or chips. Other passives can provide stacking bonuses when playing hands with less cards, making up for the lack of potential score by rewarding you for playing those easy hands and stacking your mult and chips into the stratosphere. One of my favorite combos is to run Spare Trousers with Square Joker and just spam Two Pair, stacking my scores like crazy. Add in unique modifiers on top of these passives, or the ability to make them negative and give an additional passive slot, and the possibilities for buildcrafting are endless. There’s a good reason I’ve already managed to put 180 hours into it, and happily would drop several dozen more. When the passives are interesting, each run feels like an experiment with a new angle.

While it’s hard to pick just one example of a roguelike that does this wrong, the most recent example I can think of is Haste. While its passives can come together to create some powerful combos, the fact that just about everything you can get is some variant of “10% increased boost bar” or “10% boost on close calls” makes for not much variance from run to run. Sure, I dropped the game mainly for other reasons, but the lack of interesting passives and build potential definitely didn’t do it any favors.

If a roguelike can honor and respect these four pillars, then you’re going to have a tough time not winning people over. That’s not to say it’s easy to do all of this, even with extensive playtesting, but it’s certainly possible with the right team, the right level of passion, and just the right amount of care. I just wish more teams would do it, since all of the roguelikes I listed above were roguelikes I **wanted** to like, only to feel like I was burned once I gave them a chance. I truly want more roguelikes that I can sink more time into than is perfectly reasonable. It just feels like so many slip up in one way or another and become like, well, one of the games I listed above.

Now if you’ll excuse me, I’m going to go browse Steam, find a roguelike that looks sick, buy it, then find out it manages to misunderstand one of these pillars. God I love this genre, but I so deeply wish developers would do more with the gift they’re given.
